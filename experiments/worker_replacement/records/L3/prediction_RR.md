## Part 1 — permanent refusals drop sharply but do NOT reach zero

Pre-repair descriptive: 335 allotment refusals across 18 episodes, mean 18.6 per episode.

**Prediction: mean permanent (allotment) refusals per episode falls below 5, and is non-zero in
at least one L3 episode.**

Reasoning: the manager acts on directly-asserted information. CHECK-1's +0.611 allocation effect
came from an announcement — a direct assertion, not an inference — **and I predicted that one wrong
by assuming the consumption bottleneck would suppress it.** I am not making the same error twice:
a rendered load block with explicit `held/capacity` is the strongest form of direct assertion in
this design, and I expect it to be used. The reason I do not predict zero is that load competes
with coverage, and coverage won decisively pre-repair — 54 of 58 over-concentrated IRB segments
were ones that worker covers, and the manager accepted 0.17 coverage mismatches where the
capacitated optimum accepts ~1. A manager that strongly prefers coverage will still occasionally
pay capacity for it.

## Part 2 — the DV becomes non-degenerate in a majority of episodes

Pre-repair descriptive (**not a baseline, per the Q4 ruling** — these episodes were generated by a
manager that could not see refusals): 6 of 18 episodes had ≥1 discretionary move; distribution
`{0 moves: 12, 1: 4, 2: 1, 3: 1}`.

**Prediction: ≥50% of L3 episodes show at least one discretionary move with ≥2 capacity-legal
destinations** — i.e. `n_moved_with_real_choice ≥ 1`.

Reasoning: visible refusal supplies the manager with a reason to move work it previously left
stuck. Pre-repair, work that was refused looked `ready`, so there was no signal to react to and
12 of 18 episodes contain no discretionary move at all. This is the part I most want to be right
about, because if it fails the primary DV is degenerate at this scope and L3 has established that
rather than anything about channels.

## Part 3 — forced-to-successor stays high, and CANNOT discriminate the failure mode

**Number (contaminated, see disclosure): forced-to-successor stays ≥80%.** Pre-repair it was
22→successor vs 2→incumbent. My own reasoning, which I do hold independently and which follows
from capacity rather than from LS's argument: post-swap the successor is at 0/3 while incumbents
have spent allotment, so with load visible the successor is the emptiest destination on the board.

**The part that is mine: this quantity cannot establish the brief's §7 failure mode #1
("allocating as if the predecessor remained"), however it moves.** Handing the departed worker's
queue to the empty successor is *also what a capacity-aware allocator does* — the failure mode and
the capacity-optimal action recommend the same destination, so a high value is consistent with
both and discriminates neither. If forced-to-successor rises after L1, the most likely explanation
is that the manager got better at capacity, not worse at the newcomer.

**The discriminating quantity, which does not currently exist: forced-to-successor restricted to
segments the successor does NOT cover.** Dumping in-scope work on an empty newcomer is capacity
reasoning; dumping out-of-scope work on it is failure mode #1. **Prediction on that quantity: it
stays low (<20%) and does not rise** — the manager routes by coverage, which is the one thing the
scope run established firmly.

## What would make me drop all three

**If the load block renders but the manager's reasoning never references load, capacity, or a
refusal, then none of the three numbers mean what they appear to** and the result is
rendering-without-consumption — the §89 error at the level of the whole phase. Check the
`structured_llm_response` reasoning text for references to the load or refusal blocks before any
of the above is interpreted. A drop in refusals with no reasoning that mentions them is a
coincidence until shown otherwise, and P13 applies: the verdict and its mechanism are separate
claims.
